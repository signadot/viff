The gates of a rule#

This page describes the gates field of a rule: what a gate asks, who answers it, and what a refusal, a deadline and a restart do to the question. The charter reference has the model that gates belong to.

The examples on this page are a team's releases of acme/app. Each release is a record at release.<n>, with a title, a stage and the number of changes it carries. Rules deploy a release when people say yes.

gates: whether it proceeds#

gates is a list of gates. Every gate must pass before the action fires. A rule with no gates fires without asking anybody. Each gate is {name: …, mode: auto|ask}: auto passes at once, and ask waits for an answer.

Gates are per firing. A firing is about one address, so a rule that selects twelve releases asks twelve questions.

The id of a question is made from the rule, the gate and the subject. So a rule that is evaluated again still asks once. A firing that finds its question already standing joins it, and does not post a copy. One answer releases every firing that waits on it. So a rule that waits on a person does not fill the docket.

Several gates are several approvals. A deploy that needs a sign-off from security and from a release manager is two ask gates. One gate that needs more than one person says so on the question it poses. A gate with more to say than yes or no names its own options:

  gates:
  - name: approvals
    pose: "Deploy *$[subject.value.title]* ($[subject.value.changes] changes) to staging?"
    need: 2                                    # proceed takes two ballots; one deny refuses
    deadline: {within: "24h", default: deny}   # and the clock decides silence
    answerers: [dana@example.com, group:payments]   # and whose ballots
  - name: timing
    pose: "Deploy *$[subject.value.title]* now?"
    options:
    - id: proceed
      need: 1
      says: "deploy it now"
    - id: hold
      need: 2
      says: "wait for the release call"
    - id: deny
      need: 1
      says: "drop this release"

What a question says#

A question says two things in words, for a person:

  • pose is the question, written on the gate.
  • about is the one line the subject says about itself. A subject whose data carries a says field has one, and verse copies it onto the question when it poses it (the question record).

A reader needs no grant on the subject to read either. The other fields of a gate are for the engine: they say who asked and how the question settles.

Verse expands pose at the firing, against the firing's subject, as it expands the strings of a spawn in the same firing: the subject's fields by name, and subject as ref, name and value. A pose and a prompt that say $[subject.value.title] say the same words. The question carries the result as words. If the expression does not expand, the question is still asked: verse drops the words, and a reader reads reason instead.

Options and their thresholds#

Every gate poses the same record:

  • the options it can settle on;
  • for each option, the number of ballots that settles it;
  • a deadline, with the option that the question falls to.

A gate that says none of these poses proceed and deny, at one ballot each.

Field What it says
says of an option what choosing the option means, for whoever is asked. The id is how an answer names the option, and the name the rule reads.
need the short spelling of options: [{id: proceed, need: N}, {id: deny, need: 1}]. Do not write need and options together.
options an ordered list. The first option to reach its threshold settles the question.

Only proceed lets the firing go on. Any other option is a no: the action does not run, and the gate's onDenied runs instead. In the example above, hold and deny both stop the deploy. A gate whose options leave out proceed can never let its action run.

A gate that carries any of these, or a pose, and no mode has the mode ask. Verse refuses such a gate with mode: auto, because auto asks nobody.

Who may answer#

answerers names who is asked. It holds grantees, in the grammar that a grant uses:

Grantee Who
user:<email>, or the bare email a person
user:* a whole kind
* everyone
group:<name> a group
via:verse.resolvers.<name> a resolver
$principal whom a task acts for: in an ask row of a task's autonomy only (below)

A gate that names nobody asks the docket.

$principal is whom a task acts for: whoever ran it as themselves, the asker of an ask whose then it runs, or the subject's owner for a spawn as: subject. It goes in an ask row of the task's autonomy: {match: {system: git, verb: push}, then: {ask: {answerers: [$principal]}}}. Verse puts that person in its place when it poses the question, so the record names them. It is one answerer with one ballot: answerers: [$principal, group:payments], need: 2 takes the person and one ballot from payments.

A rule's own gate is asked by the rule, and a rule acts for nobody. So verse refuses $principal on a rule's gate at install. It also refuses it in a spawn's autonomy unless the spawn says as: subject. And it refuses the spawn when the subject's owner is the system.

When verse poses the question, it writes answerers as the question's read-write grant, in place of the docket's default. Each answerer has one ballot. need says how many must answer:

  • need: 1 is a race;
  • need equal to the number of answerers is all of them.

The owner of the charter co-owns what its rule asked, so the owner may answer without being named.

Verse refuses at install a gate that nobody could settle:

  • a threshold above the number of answerers named;
  • an answerer that the grammar does not read;
  • a group or a resolver that does not stand.

Tasks and gates says who may answer, and questions has the record that answerers becomes.

answerersFrom: whom the record names#

answerersFrom: answerers reads the answerers from a field of the subject at the firing, not from the rule. So one rule can ask, for each record, whoever that record names.

  gates:
  - name: approval
    pose: "Deploy *$[subject.value.title]*?"
    answerersFrom: approvers      # {title: …, approvers: dana@example.com}

So each release names who approves it. The field holds one name or a list, in the same grammar as answerers. For a field deeper in the record, write it dotted (review.by).

A record without the field changes nothing: the gate's own answerers apply, or the docket's default where the gate names none.

What a record names is checked as answerers is checked at install. An empty list, a name the grammar does not read, or fewer people than need makes a question nobody could settle. Verse asks nobody in that case.

labels: what the ask says about itself#

labels: {team: payments, kind: deploy} goes onto the gate's question. Whoever watches questions can match on them: a resolver, or a charter that posts each question carrying a slack: <channel> label to that Slack channel. A label is a name and a value that the charter's author and the reader agree on. Verse itself reads nothing into a label and routes nothing by one. A name holding a space is refused at install. An ask row of a task's autonomy takes the same field.

The tally#

The question's record keeps the tally. The engine does not count.

  • An answer is a ballot on the record.
  • The question settles in the same write as the ballot that reaches a threshold.
  • An answerer who casts twice replaces their own ballot. The ballot does not count twice.
  • A ballot says how its caster was authenticated: authenticated is the identity provider that verified them, or asserted for one who was whoever their request said (who a request is from). A ballot that a resolver cast for somebody names the resolver in via, and has no authenticated. A ballot a trusted iod relayed for a person names the iod in via and the iod's provider in authenticated (a person relayed by a trusted iod).

The engine asks exactly what was written, and acts on the one answer that comes back.

The answer is written first#

The answer that settles a question is written on its record as followed before anything else happens: before an ask runs the row of its on that the answer meets, and before the firing or the task hears it. It holds the answer as the answered record spells it (verdict, by, at, and via, why, say, edit and authenticated where the answer has them). The first one written settles the question, so two answers landing together cannot disagree. The second is refused with 409, and the refusal names whose answer stands. The same answer, by the same answerer, again is a retry of the first.

A delivery that fails leaves the question pending and says why on the answer, as undelivered. The next answer delivers it. So does posing the question again. An answer that nothing is left to hear, the awaiter or the task's decision having gone, is answered 202 with the answer stands. It is delivered when the question is next asked.

Waiting for a fact is not a gate. A rule that acts once CI is green says so in when: (the condition).

onDenied: what a no means#

A denied firing writes nothing. So the subject stays in the exact state that asked the question. The next commit that touches the subject selects it again, and the rule fires again. The gate asks again: a new question, with an id of its own, to somebody who already answered.

The latch of a well-formed rule cannot help. The latch is in the action, and on a deny the action never runs. So the gate says what its own refusal means:

  gates:
  - name: approval
    mode: ask
    onDenied:
      patch: { stage: rejected }

onDenied is an ordinary action: patch, derive, spawn, and their completion hooks. Verse applies it to the same subject the firing was about. It is a field of the gate, not of the action. It is not a completion hook, and it needs no spawn of its own.

Only a refusal that settled the question runs onDenied. The no may come from one person, from a threshold of people, or from the clock's default. onDenied does not run when:

  • an answerer could not be reached;
  • a question was withdrawn because its subject left the selection.

In those cases nobody said anything. A rejection written for them would put a decision in the store that nobody made.

A gate may leave out onDenied: "ask again the next time somebody touches it" is a real policy. So a gate without onDenied gets a note at install, not a refusal. The install prints it under the line that says what was installed, as note: rule <name>: ….

To make a no final without changing the state of the subject itself, use a marker:

  condition:
    subject:
      path: "release.*"
      value: !and
      - { stage: ready }
      - !not { blocked: true }
  gates:
  - name: approval
    mode: ask
    onDenied:
      patch: { blocked: true }

Removing the marker then opens the question again.

A gate starts nothing of its own. What a rule does about a yes is its action, and what a no means for the subject is onDenied. The on of an ask is a person's alone.

A deadline settles#

deadline: {within: "24h", default: deny} settles the question on deny when the time passes and no option is at its threshold. The question records by: deadline, and the firing sees a refusal.

Verse writes the instant on the record once, when it first poses the question. Every later posing uses that instant. A restart sets the clock to the written instant, not to a new duration. So a deadline that passed while verse was down fires as verse starts again.

The default is an ordinary option. A ballot for it counts toward its own threshold, like any other ballot. So a deny that needs one ballot settles on one deny, before the clock.

A default left empty settles on nothing (verdict: none). The firing reads that as a refusal nobody made.

deadline: {atFrom: by, default: proceed} takes the instant from a field of the subject instead. The field holds an RFC 3339 date. That is how a record says "by Friday", rather than a duration from whenever verse posed the question. Verse reads it at the firing, and again when it posts the question.

  • A record without the field has no deadline.
  • A record whose date does not parse is a question that nobody is asked.
  • While the question stands, editing the date moves its deadline. Verse does not ask the question again.
  • A settled question keeps the instant it settled under.

Whoever may write the record may move the date.

Do not write within and atFrom together. at is the instant the engine worked out and wrote; a rule never writes it. An ask row of a task's autonomy has within only, because there is no record for it to read a date from.

A question outlives the verse that asked it#

The question's record, its options and every ballot are data in the store. So a restart finds the question exactly where it stood.

The awaiter does not survive a restart. The awaiter is the part of the firing that waits for the answer. A question with no awaiter would be the worst entry on the docket: somebody answers it, and nothing moves.

So at startup verse evaluates each rule again. The action never ran, so a rule that still selects the subject poses the same question again. It joins the record that stands, with its options and every ballot already cast. A rule that no longer selects the subject poses nothing. While verse runs, a question whose subject leaves what its rule reads is withdrawn, and an answer to it decides nothing.

Posing again cannot make a second question, so it is safe beside a rule that also fires again:

  • the question id is derived from the rule, the gate and the subject, and a firing joins the question that stands open under it;
  • a question settles once, and a firing after an answer asks a new question under <id>-2, <id>-3, and so on;
  • the spawn id is deterministic per (chain, subject).

One question, and one task, however many paths reach it. A question posed again while an answer stands on it delivers that answer (the answer is written first), and the firing hears it.

A scope is not covered. Its questions are in the scope's own store, and nothing lists them at startup. Charters and claims in a scope have the same gap.

If verse stops between settling a question and starting the work, the spawn is lost. The record is answered, so nothing poses it again. The same gap exists between patch and spawn inside one action. An action is not atomic: a crash in the middle leaves the half that landed.

A gate's answer applies to the world it was asked in#

While a question stands, the firing watches the subject and what is beneath it. At every change it asks whether the rule would still select the subject. When the rule would not, the question no longer matters, and verse withdraws it:

  • the record says status: orphaned, with a why;
  • there is no verdict and no action;
  • onDenied does not run, because a withdrawn question is not a refusal.

An answer that arrives after the subject left the selection is the same case. The firing withdraws, and does not act.

The withdrawal happens at once, at the change that made the question not matter. Nobody has to answer, and nothing has to restart.

The check has two limits.

  • It asks only about the subject, not the cause. A rule fired by an on edge may still select its subject and never see that edge again. So a question that stands does not mean the rule will fire again.
  • When it cannot tell, it lets the question stand. It cannot tell when the store is unreadable, or when a read of the predicate fails.

A credential for what a gate let through#

A gate stops verse's own work. Work that reaches a system verse does not own, such as a cloud account or a cluster, is stopped only if that system refuses what no gate approved. A credential makes that possible. It is a short-lived token that verse signs for the task of a rule whose gate passed. You set up the outside system to accept such a token and nothing else.

- name: deploy
  condition: {subject: {path: "release.*", value: {stage: ready}}}
  gates: [{name: go, mode: ask}]
  action:
    patch: {stage: deploying}
    spawn:
      script: |
        set -e
        cd app && make build
        AWS_WEB_IDENTITY_TOKEN_FILE=$VERSE_CREDENTIAL_FILE aws s3 sync build s3://releases
      environment:
        vars: {AWS_ROLE_ARN: "arn:aws:iam::123456789012:role/releases"}
        systems:
          network: {mode: open}
          git: {repo: acme/app, fetch: {}}
      credential: {audience: sts.amazonaws.com}

The runner checks out acme/app, and the script builds it and syncs the build to S3. AWS takes the token from the file and the role from AWS_ROLE_ARN.

The verse is an issuer. Its config names the address and the signing key (auth.issuer). The outside system reads the verse's public keys from that address, and checks each token's signature for itself.

The audience is a system outside verse. A credential whose audience is a registered runner's subject is refused at the spawn. A runner admits only the token verse signs as itself, sub: verse, so a credential would not open its door.

sub is what the outside system matches on. It is the rule's address, then how the gate was passed:

sub the gate was passed
verse.trigger.rule.deploy:ballots on the ballots that people cast
verse.trigger.rule.deploy:deadline on its deadline's default, with nobody's answer deciding it
verse.trigger.rule.deploy:auto as a gate written mode: auto, which asks nobody
  • sub names no task and no subject. So you grant access once for a rule, and it holds for every firing of it.
  • A system that requires a person's answer grants only the :ballots form. If the rule's gate is later changed to auto, its tokens say :auto and that system refuses them.
  • A rule with several gates says what its questions came to. It is :deadline if any gate that asked was settled by its deadline, and :ballots if every gate that asked was answered. It is :auto only where no gate asked.
  • A rule in a package has the package in its address: verse.trigger.rule."<package>:<name>":ballots.

Work nobody approves says so. A rule whose work should reach the outside system without asking anybody writes a gate that says that:

- name: deploy-staging
  condition: {subject: {path: "release.*", value: {stage: built}}}
  gates: [{name: unattended, mode: auto}]
  action:
    patch: {stage: staging}
    spawn:
      script: |
        set -e
        cd app && make build
        AWS_WEB_IDENTITY_TOKEN_FILE=$VERSE_CREDENTIAL_FILE aws s3 sync build s3://staging
      environment:
        vars: {AWS_ROLE_ARN: "arn:aws:iam::123456789012:role/staging"}
        systems:
          network: {mode: open}
          git: {repo: acme/app, fetch: {}}
      credential: {audience: sts.amazonaws.com}

Its token says :auto. It promises only that this rule fired and verse started this task. Nobody approved it. So whoever may write the subject can cause it. Grant :auto in the outside system only what that is safe for.

The other claims are for the outside system's audit log.

Claim What it holds
iss the verse's address as an issuer
aud the audience that the spawn declared
iat, nbf, exp when it was signed, and when it stops being good: ten minutes later
jti an id of this token alone
task the task's id
rule, charter the rule's address, and its package's where it is in one
subject the address the firing was about
principal who the task acts as
gates for each gate of the rule: gate (its name) and passed (ballots, deadline or auto). A gate that asked also has question (the question's address) and answerers.

Each of answerers is {principal, authenticated}: who cast a ballot for the option the gate settled on, and how they were authenticated. An answer that a resolver gave for somebody has via and no authenticated; one a trusted iod relayed for a person has both. A gate settled by its deadline may have none.

The task's record says a credential was minted. It has credential: {audience, sub, jti, expires}, and never the token. jti is the token's own id, which the outside system's log has too.

The task gets the token as a file. The runner writes it outside the task's workspace, for the task's user alone, and sets VERSE_CREDENTIAL_FILE in the task's shell to the file's path. The token is never a value in the environment. AWS's and GCP's tools take such a token from a file. The file is removed when the task ends.

Who gets no credential. Verse refuses the charter at install where it can tell, and fails the spawn where it cannot. Either way it says why.

  • A spawn that declares none gets none.
  • A rule that writes no gate. A credential says which gate let the work through, so there has to be one, and mode: auto is one.
  • A completion hook's spawn, or a step of a seq. They start after the answer was given. Make that step a rule of its own, with its own gate.
  • A verse with no auth.issuer.
  • An audience with an expression in it. The audience is declared in the charter, and the subject's text cannot choose it.

A token is minted once, when the task is spawned. Verse cannot take a token back, so a cancelled task could still act for up to ten minutes. After that, the outside system refuses the token. So keep the gated work short, and gate it as its own rule.

gating#

gating:
  gates:
  - name: ship
    mode: ask

gating holds the gates of a rule. gates: at the top level means gating: {gates: …}. Verse refuses a rule that writes both; it does not merge them. Verse refuses a resolver: under gating or on a spawn. A gate names its answerers, and whoever is asked grants the resolver that answers for them.

Permissions reference#

This page is the detail of permissions. The model defines the words, and Permissions, walked shows them by example. This page has:

  • the grammar of names;
  • the fields of a record;
  • what verse checks, and when;
  • who a request is from, and accounts in other systems;
  • what each act writes.

The reason for each rule is in the source, beside the code that enforces it (pkg/entity/attribution.go, internal/versed/perms.go, internal/versed/place.go).

The grammar#

principal ::= user:<email>          # a person
            | agent:<name>          # an agent driving verse through the api
            | runner:<identity>     # a runner
            | system:<part>         # verse itself
            | via:<address>         # something installed, acting as whoever installed it
            | pair:<asker> <rule>   # a rule's task, acting for whoever wrote what it is about
grantee   ::= principal
            | *                     # everyone, a read that names nobody included
            | <kind>:*              # every principal of the kind
            | group:<name>          # the group's members, each as themselves
            | audience:<address>    # whoever holds the same level at the address
            | answering:<address>   # the resolvers granted `answer` at the address
            | <email>               # shorthand for user:<email>, at the keyboard only

Every place that takes a name reads this grammar, and refuses what it does not read:

  • -as;
  • the config's user: and agent:;
  • a grant line and a child-grants;
  • a group's members;
  • a gate's answerers, and an ask row's;
  • a resolver grant's answer line.

The rules of the grammar:

  • A bare email is read as user:<email> and stored with its kind. Any other bare word names nothing. A bare verse.… address names nothing too.
  • A request presents user:, agent: or runner:. via:, pair: and system: are names that verse makes. Verse refuses a request that carries one of them with 400, and the same for a name the grammar does not read.
  • A request that is not a read presents one. Verse refuses it with 400 when it names nobody. Some routes name their own party (routes that name their own party, in the repository).
  • Verse lists a principal the first time it presents itself, under verse.principal.<kind>.<name>.
  • pair:<asker> <rule> is the task of a rule whose spawn says as: subject. The asker is the owner on record of the subject, and the rule is named as it acts, via:<its address>. The task acts on the asker's authority alone, and the rule's name says what it acted through. A space separates the two, since no principal holds one. It is never a grantee.
  • system:<part> names the part of verse that wrote, such as system:docket for questions. All the parts are one owner (the system parts, in the repository).

How a grantee matches.

grantee admits
<kind>:<id> the principal named exactly that, and anything installed that acts as them
<kind>:* any principal of the kind, and anything installed that acts as one
via:<address> the installed thing at that address and nothing else. It does not admit the thing's owner. A trailing * covers what acts beneath (via:verse.iods.*). Only a rule, an iod or a resolver is named this way. A runner acts as runner:<identity>.
group:<name> the group's members, each matched as above. A member may be another group, and verse follows it. Verse does not enter a group it already met on the walk, so groups that name each other do not loop. They admit exactly the members found along the walk.
audience:<address> whoever holds, at the address, the level this grantee is listed under: in read, whoever may read the address, and in read-write, whoever may write it. It is allowed in read and read-write only, since owning a place is not owning what is about it. Verse follows it one address deep: an audience: met while answering another admits nobody. It is never a principal.
answering:<address> whoever holds answer at the address: the resolvers answering for a person at verse.for.<person>, or for a place. It is allowed in read only. An ask's read names answering: for each of its parties, so the resolvers serving it can read it. Verse follows it one address deep, as it does audience:. It is never a principal.
* everyone

A stored name that the grammar does not read admits nobody. Nothing rewrites it. verse status counts each check that meets one under perms.unparsed, and versed logs it once per record.

Records#

Records live at verse.perms.node.<address>. The root's record is at verse.perms.root. Only verse writes records, and only the system reads them directly. Everybody else reads and sets them with verse perms.

field meaning
owner the principal who owns the node. It is absent on a record that only holds grants (a seed row).
grants a list of lines. [] grants nothing.
child-grants permission levels mapped to grantees: the grants a record made beneath this node starts with. Verse copies it when that record is written, unless the creation gives grants of its own. It is not a fallback: a change to it touches nothing that exists. The nearest child-grants up the tree applies, and the root's is the last. It never holds answer, which only the party a resolver answers for can grant.
under an address. Whoever co-owns that node co-owns this one. Verse writes it on a question: what asked it.

A grant line:

field meaning
read, read-write, control, answer grantees of that level
child-owner grantees who may create beneath, and own what they create. It is always about what is beneath, so it takes no below. There is no grant of owner at a node.
below: true the line covers what is beneath the node, and not the node
at: [segs…] the line is about another address, with at most a trailing *. Without at, the line is about the node the record is on, as nearly every line is.

A grant does not reach through an owner record. Verse walks up from a node. The grants on each record apply, up to and including the first record that names an owner. The grants above that record do not apply.

A group is verse.principal.group.<name>. It has members, a list of grantees, and whoever made it owns it. Verse reads the membership when it checks the group.

Levels#

A permission level is what a principal may do at a node.

level admits
read reading the node and what is beneath it
read-write writing into the node, with fields removed as well as set
owner everything: deleting the node (which takes what is beneath it), replacing it whole, and setting its record. The owner on record and the owners above hold it. child-owner gives it only on a creation.
control steering work: run, cancel, pause, resume, say, change an autonomy, suspend. An owner by record holds it, and so does whoever it is granted to.
answer which resolvers may answer for the party the node stands for: a place's owner, at the place, or a person, at verse.for.<person>. It is held only by being named: owning the node gives none of it, and a resolver gets none through whoever installed it. An owner above may take it back but not give it, and no child-grants passes it down. It reaches beneath its node as a grant does. verse resolver grant writes it.

A spawn carries the identity of whoever runs it, and verse writes the task's record as that identity. That write is the check for run.

A refusal that says "needs owner" about a creation means that no child-owner line reaches there.

Creating, and taking a place#

  • Who owns a created node. A writer may hold owner where they write, by owning above or by a child-owner line. That writer owns what they create. Verse writes a record if the writer is not the owner above, or if the write gives the node grants (verse data … -put … -grants). A writer who holds only read-write creates the node too, but the node belongs to the owner of the place. Verse writes no record, and refuses grants on the write.
  • Where the record goes. At the topmost node that the write brought into being. The walk up stops at anything already there, as a node or as a place somebody took. A node created with grants has its own record too, written in the same commit as the node.
  • child-owner applies to a creation only. Verse commits a write that only that line admits only if nothing stands where the write lands: at the node, and at each node its value reaches. Of two people who make one node at once, one owns it and verse refuses the other. Where something does stand, verse admits the write on whatever else the writer holds there, or refuses it and names the owner.
  • Taking a place (verse perms <path> where no record stands) needs owner there. Through a child-owner line, that means nothing may stand at the node yet. Under verse, a place can be taken only where something stands, and only by its owner. So nobody can reserve a name the system has not used yet.
  • First come, first served. A child-owner line says who may create beneath a node. It does not say which name each of them may create. Where spaces grants it to user:*, anybody may take spaces.sally. The owner of spaces co-owns spaces.sally and can remove it. Nothing maps a name to a principal (b737wbpb).
  • Not above somebody else's place. owner from a child-owner line does not apply at a node that has another principal's record beneath it. The refusal names the place in the way. Ownership by record is unchanged: the owner of spaces co-owns every space in it.
  • A task's id names one party's work. Verse refuses a spawn under an id that already stands as somebody else's, a rule's spawn included.
  • Deleting a node forgets its record and every record beneath it.

What is checked on a write#

Every write verse makes for anybody goes through one view of the store, and that view acts as a principal. So verse checks compare-and-swap writes and its own projections too, and the code that makes the write does not need to know. A refusal is a 403 with the check's words, counted under perms.refused.

  • The node written is the whole address the request spells. The api's sub beneath an address is part of the address where it spells names: {at: team, sub: c.secret} is a write at team.c.secret.
  • Every node the value reaches is checked, wherever a record or a grant could change the answer from the node above. {c: {secret: x}} written at team is also a write at team.c. A !delete inside a value replaces its node whole, and so does a value that is not an object. Either needs owner wherever something beneath could be somebody else's.
  • A writer may read what the write asks about. Verse refuses a when: precondition on a node the writer may not read, as it would refuse a read of the node. Verse checks a pattern field by field, so it never refuses a pattern for what the pattern does not mention.
  • A removal is checked before it starts. Removing a rule, a level, a package, an iod or a runner needs owner on it. Verse checks that before it stops or deletes anything. Replacing a rule or a level is a write over what stands: the thing's record and grants stay. A co-owner who edits a rule does not become its owner.
  • A charter file is applied as one step. A refusal anywhere puts back what stood, content and record.
  • Two checks come before their write, because what follows cannot be undone. Verse checks an answer before it delivers it to the work, and a control call before it relays it.

What is checked on a read#

  • Verse refuses a read of one thing the reader may not read, as it refuses a write.
  • A listing, a replay, a watch and a snapshot leave out what may not be read. The same holds inside a payload: a node under a record the reader may not read is left out of what holds it. Verse counts what it leaves out under perms.hidden.
  • Verse refuses a listing or a watch at an address the reader may not read into, as a whole.
  • A task is read as its record is. The task's prompt, its transcript, its result and the answers waiting for its worker each need read on the task's record, verse.task.instance.<id>. The routes, and the one read that is not checked, are in reads by a worker, in the repository.
  • There is one gap, one commit wide. The record follows the write it records. So the commit that creates a node reaches a live watch before the record that would hide it.
  • A rule reads as its owner. Verse narrows three things to what the rule's owner may read: the snapshot that a condition is asked of, the snapshot that a reference resolves against, and the commit that wakes the rule. Verse refuses at install a rule that reads the records themselves, unless the rule is the system's.

Installed things#

A rule, an iod, a resolver and a schedule act as via:<address>. That principal is the owner on the record at that address. Where the address has no record, it is nobody. It does not fall back to the owner above, because above is verse's own half, which is the system's.

  • An install leaves its installer's record, or verse refuses it with 409.
  • Whose a rule is, is said on its record and nowhere else. A rule's own data carries no installer.
  • The deployment's charter is installed as the system, and fires as the system.
  • No rule but the system's writes under verse. Verse refuses a patch of a subject there, or a derive into it, with 403. It refuses at install where the rule names the address, and at the write. A rule may select a subject there and spawn about it.
  • A charter package takes the address it is installed at for its installer. An iod takes the nodes its claims name in the same way.

How each installed thing gets its record, and what the system makes under verse, is in installed things and their records, in the repository.

Questions#

A gate's question is an ask, at verse.ask.<id>, with the docket's record. Its under is what asked it. A person's ask has its asker's record instead, which verse ask writes with who reads it and who answers it (asking).

  • Who may read it. A question about something is read by whoever may read that thing: its read line is audience:<subject>. A question about nothing is read as the child-grants on verse.ask say.
  • Who may answer. Whoever co-owns the question through under: the task's owner, or the charter's owner. Also whoever the question's own read-write line names. Verse takes that line from the first of these that applies:
  • for a rule's gate, the gate's answerers;
  • for a question a task raises, the answerers of the autonomy row that asked; else whom the worker named when it asked; else whom the task acts for ($principal), where it acts for somebody;
  • for a question about something, audience:<subject>: whoever may write it;
  • the child-grants on verse.ask (read-write: [user:*, agent:*]).
  • Nobody answers what they cannot read. A question naming answerers who may not read its subject is refused when it is asked: the firing or the task that asked gets the refusal as its error, and verse ask gets 403. A whole kind (user:*) passes only where the subject grants read to that kind or to *. An answer from somebody who may no longer read the subject is refused with 403.

A runner's grant is on its task, and reaches no question. - Through a resolver, an answer is its party's: a resolver answering for a person or a place that granted it answer answers as them, held to their line (Resolvers). Nothing moves the line. - Asked again after it stood orphaned, a question keeps its id, and who may answer belongs to the new asking. A question that was answered is not asked again: a gate that fires after the answer asks a new question, under an id of its own. - Answerable once posed. Posing is two writes: the record, then the question. An answer that arrives before both waits a moment, and then gets 503 … still being posed. - By whoever made the request. A ballot is keyed by the principal the check admitted, and a record names that principal. This holds for an answer, a say, a cancel, a change of autonomy, and the principal a spawn is answerable to. need counts one ballot for each. - What a worker reports is not evidence of who answered. For a task on a remote runner, who works the task is who claimed it. How verse reads what such a worker sends is in a relayed task's frames, in the repository.

Who a request is from#

In short: a request is made as a principal, and verse decides which one from what the request carries.

  • A name alone is taken as given, where the mode allows.
  • A token from an identity provider the deployment names is verified. The principal is whoever the token's account is bound to.
  • An iod of the deployment presents its own token. When it acts for a person, it also names the person's account, and verse finds the person through that account's binding.
  • A runner presents its own token too, the one its registration names by its subject, and acts as itself.

Bindings are records at verse.identity.<provider>.account.<id>. Only the system writes them, from evidence it checked itself (accounts in other systems).

A request says who makes it in one of three ways.

  • A token. Authorization: Bearer <id_token> carries an OpenID Connect id_token from an identity provider the deployment names (auth.principal in its config). Verse verifies it, and the request is made as the person it proves.
  • A name. Verse-Principal: <principal> is taken as given. It is user:, agent: or runner:.
  • An iod's token, and a person it relays. An iod of the deployment presents its own token (iods at the door). When it acts for a person, it also sends that person's name and the account the person is: Verse-For-Principal: <provider>:<id>. Verse admits the request as the person only through the account's binding, and only from an iod the deployment trusts for that provider (a person relayed by a trusted iod).

A token is checked before the name is read:

  1. Its iss names one of the deployment's providers.
  2. Its signature verifies against the keys that provider publishes. Verse reads a provider's discovery document the first time a token names it. It reads the keys again when it meets a key id it has not seen.
  3. Its aud holds the provider's clientId, and it is in date (exp, nbf).
  4. The principal is the binding, not the claim. The token's sub is an account, and the binding at verse.identity.<provider>.account.<sub> says which principal that account is. So a person whose email changes at the provider keeps their principal.
  5. First sight. An account with no binding is bound to user:<email> when the token carries an email with email_verified: true in one of the provider's domains. The email is lower-cased. The binding's proof is {method: oidc, iss, sub, iat, at}, and its claimant is the provider. Verse never stores the token.
a request with is answered
a token that passes as the principal its account is bound to
a token that fails any check above 401, saying which. It never falls back to the name beside it.
a token, at a verse that names no provider or whose mode is off 401
a token whose provider cannot be reached 503. Requests with no token are still served.
a token that passes, and a Verse-Principal naming somebody else 403
an iod's token as the iod, under every mode
an iod's token, a person's name and their account, from an iod trusted for the account's provider as the person, through the binding (below)
an iod's token and a person's name, with no account or from an iod not trusted for it 403
a registered runner's token as the runner, under every mode (below)
a registered runner's token and a name that is not the runner's, with no task 403
a registered runner's token and a task it hosts (Verse-Task) as the task's owner, through the task's record (below)
a registered runner's token and an account to relay (Verse-For-Principal) 403: a runner relays a task, never an account, and the request is not carried out as the runner instead
a Verse-Task beside any token but a runner's, or beside none 403 or 401: only the runner hosting a task carries a request for it
no token, under off or optional as the Verse-Principal it names, or as nobody
no token and a runner: name, under every mode 401: the runner presents its token
no token and a user: name, under required 401, saying to log in
no token and an agent: name, under required as that name
no token and no name, under required as nobody: it reads what * holds, and writes nothing

auth.principal.mode says how strictly the verse requires a token.

mode a person is
off whoever the request says. No token is verified.
optional whoever their token proves, or whoever the request says where it carries none
required whoever their token proves, and nobody else

Verse verifies people, iods and registered runners, not agents. The modes above verify people, auth.iod verifies the deployment's own iods, and a runner proves itself by the subject its registration names (runners at the door). An agent: principal is taken as given, under every mode. An agent may read bindings, so required does not close the door to everybody who is not verified.

An asserted binding is not answered to an iod's lookup under required, until a verified caller proves the account again.

Under optional, a user: that carries no token is also taken as given. A binding records which kind of caller proved it (claimant), so a reader can tell a verified name from an asserted one.

GET /whoami answers who the request is from and how verse knows: {principal, by}. by is the provider that verified the caller, or asserted. Where an iod relayed the caller, via names it. A request that names nobody is answered {}.

Your account#

command does
verse account lists the accounts in other systems that are bound to you
verse account login [<provider>] logs in to an identity provider in the CLI's config, and keeps the tokens. The provider may be left off when the config names one.
verse account -link <provider> binds an account in another system to who you are. At an identity provider, it logs in there and presents the token as evidence; the login in use does not change. At a provider whose accounts an iod proves (slack, github), it claims the account and prints the words to say from it; -account <login> names a GitHub login. For GitHub it then waits, asking verify until verse has read the words in a comment, and ends when the account is bound or the claim expires; a login already bound to you is said so, and not claimed.
verse whoami prints who the verse takes you for, and how: user:ana@example.com (google), or (asserted)
verse account logout [<provider>] drops the login toward this verse: every provider's, or the one named
  • How it logs in. Where the provider offers the device authorization grant (RFC 8628), the CLI prints a URL and a code, and waits while you confirm the code in any browser. Otherwise it prints the provider's login URL and listens on a loopback port for the browser to come back (authorization code with PKCE). It asks for the scopes openid and email, and for offline_access where the provider lists it.
  • What is kept. The id_token, when it expires, the refresh token if the provider gave one, and the principal the verse answered. There is one entry for each verse address and provider.
  • What is sent. After a login, every request carries the id_token and no Verse-Principal. The login comes before user in the config.
  • When the CLI acts as somebody else. With -as, or with agent in the config, the CLI sends that name alone and no token. A token beside another name is refused.
  • Renewal. An id_token that has expired is renewed with the refresh token before the request is sent. Where it cannot be renewed, the command fails and says to log in again. It is not sent under the config's user instead. A provider that does not answer within 30 seconds fails the command too.
  • A login the verse refuses is not kept. verse account login asks the verse who the token proves before it reports success. That is also when a first sight is bound.

Iods at the door#

An iod is a program of the deployment's own. It proves which iod it is with the token its runtime gives it. auth.iod in the config says what that token is:

  • On Kubernetes, the pod's projected service-account token, asked for with the audience verse. Its issuer is the cluster's own. On EKS that is https://oidc.eks.<region>.amazonaws.com/id/<id>, which is public. On minikube or kubeadm it is the API server itself, behind the cluster's CA, which auth.iod.ca names. Verse verifies it as it verifies a person's token, against the issuer's published keys, with aud checked against auth.iod.audience.
  • With no cluster, a token in a file the deployment wrote. Verse reads the file at start and keeps the token's hash.

The config says which iod a token is: auth.iod.accounts maps a token's subject (system:serviceaccount:<namespace>:<name>) to the agent: the iod acts as and the name it is installed under. At every start, verse writes the accounts to verse.identity.iod.account.<sub> and a static token's hash to verse.identity.iod.static.<hash>, where anyone can read them. An iod is never bound at first sight. A verified iod is its principal under every mode, and whoami says by: iod.

Runners at the door#

A runner proves which runner it is the same way, with the workload token its runtime gives it, from the same issuer (auth.iod.issuer, audience verse). What differs is where verse is told which runner a token is: not in the config, but on the runner's registration, whose subject is the token's (verse runner add -subject system:serviceaccount:<namespace>:<name>). Whoever writes the registration vouches for the runner, once, and that is the whole decision. Who that may be is the record's to say: the seed holds owner beneath verse.runners out to everyone, the open verse's answer, and a deployment narrows it in the record, as a deployment's repopulate script does to user:admin@verse with everyone still reading. What a runner proved is recorded as verse, whoever asked.

The door keeps one table for both: a workload token's subject is looked up among the iods first and the registrations second. A match is runner:<identity>, the identity on the registration, under every mode; whoami says by: runner. A runner's own requests are made as itself, so its token beside a name that is not its own is refused.

A task's request is carried by its runner. A verb a task performs on verse is the task owner's act, and the runner hosting the task carries it: the runner's token, and Verse-Task naming the task. Verse admits it through the task's record, which verse wrote at the spawn. The record's runnerName is the runner's registration, its runnerSubject is the subject the runner proved, and the task has not ended. The request is then made as the record's owner: whoever ran the task, or the owner of the charter whose rule spawned it, or the person a rule's as: subject acts for. whoami says by: runner and via: runner:<identity>. A runner carries what the verse system's tools do on verse, raising an ask (POST /asks, verse-ask-create), reading the owner's asks (GET /asks, verse-ask-get and verse-ask-list), reading, writing and removing the verse at an address (GET /state, POST /entity, DELETE /entity; verse-data-get, -put, -rm), reading the charters, checking an install, installing and removing them (GET /charter, POST /charter/check, POST /charter, DELETE /charter; verse-charter-get, -check, -install, -remove), and whoami; any other route named with a task is refused, since what follows a task's ask is held within the task only where the ask is raised, and what a task's charter spawns only where the charter is installed (a charter a task installs). A refusal at this door carries WWW-Authenticate, so a runner tells its own fault from a refusal of what it carried. The runner names nobody, and a Verse-Principal beside the task names that owner or is refused. A registration rewritten under the same name with another subject carries nothing of the tasks the old one hosted, and a task the system owns is carried for nobody. The local runner verse up starts is a registered runner, and carries its tasks' acts this way too.

A runner is never taken at its word. A request naming a runner: with no token that proves it is refused with 401, whatever the mode, on the name alone and without reading the registrations: the runner presents its token, and a request made as it by hand cannot. Every registration names a subject, so every runner has a token to present. A verse's embedded runner is no registration, and never calls verse's door.

A static token proves a runner off Kubernetes. A bearer that is not a JWT, and is no iod's static token, is looked up by its hash among the registrations, whose subject names a static token as sha256:<hex> (verse runner add -token-file). A match is the runner, as a workload token's is. Verse holds only the hash, and each kind of proof finds only its own kind of registration.

A registrations read that did not happen refuses. Where they cannot be read, the door answers a runner's token 503, rather than taking it as one nobody registered.

The registration also holds the runner to its subject when verse asks it what it offers (runners): a runner that does not prove it is the one registered takes no spawn. And verse proves itself to the runner the other way round, with a token it signs as the issuer auth.issuer makes it, minted for that runner's subject alone, with sub: verse. The runner admits that subject alone, since the same issuer signs gate credentials too, for an audience a charter declares.

A person relayed by a trusted iod#

An iod that acts for a person does not act on its own authority. It relays a person the external system identified: the Slack member who typed an answer, or the GitHub login who commented. Verse admits such a request as the person when all of these hold, in order:

  1. The bearer verifies as an iod.
  2. That iod is in the provider's iods (auth.principal.providers.<provider>.iods): the deployment said this iod's word counts for that provider's accounts, and for nobody else's.
  3. The request names the account, Verse-For-Principal: <provider>:<id>, beside the person's name in Verse-Principal. The account is what the iod saw; the name is who it concluded.
  4. The account resolves to the presented person: through its binding at verse.identity.<provider>.account.<id>, or, on first sight, where the provider has domains and the person's email is in one. A first sight writes the binding with the provider as its claimant, the iod as via, and proof: {method: relay, iod, account, at}. An account bound to somebody else is refused with 409.
  5. The act is one a trusted iod does for a person: a read, or answering a question, saying on it, and steering a task or a flow. A provider's entry may say acts: any.

The request then goes on as the person. The permission check, the write and the record are theirs, and a ballot records authenticated: <provider> and via: <the iod>. Each part of the trust is stated: verse checked that the iod is the iod, the external system checked the person, and the deployment said the iod counts for these accounts.

It is not delegation between people. A person's token beside another person's name is still refused. An iod presenting an agent: or a runner: is refused. A provider with no domains admits a relayed person only through a binding that already stands. A stricter deployment wants that: a person logs in once, links their account, and only then answers from Slack.

Accounts in other systems#

An account binding says which account in another system is which principal. With it, an iod acts as the person who wrote something there, without keeping a table of its own.

In short: a person binds their own accounts, one per system, and the system writes the record.

  • For Slack and GitHub, the proof is a nonce. verse account -link <provider> gives it, and the person says it from the account. For Slack, the iod that heard it reports it. For GitHub, the CLI asks verse to find it in the comments the github iod reflects. Either way the binding is made.
  • For an identity provider, the proof is a verified token.

A binding says how it was proved (proof) and how the person who proved it was authenticated (claimant), so a reader can tell a verified name from an asserted one. One account is one principal, and a person may hold many accounts.

An account is keyed by its issuer and the issuer's own id for it, (iss, sub), as OIDC keys one. Bindings are kept per provider: the name a deployment gives an issuer, or gives the iods it trusts where no issuer is involved. There are two providers of that second kind:

  • github: its iss is https://github.com, and its sub is the user's number. A login can be renamed, so it is kept only as a hint, and a binding survives a rename.
  • slack: its accounts are the workspace's members, as the slack iod names them. One is bound when the person says a nonce to the app (heard, below), or, where a domain admits it, when the iod first relays the person.

The person claims, and the system binds. Only system:identity writes a binding, from evidence verse read itself. A binding is not on the person's own records, because a person could rewrite what they own.

  • The proof of a GitHub account is a comment that says /verse link <nonce> on a line of its own. The claimed login must write it, on a repository that a trusted github iod watches, and it must carry the author's number (authorId). Until the iod has written that comment into the store, verify answers 404 and the claim still stands. An expired claim answers 410, and is removed. Two routes read the same comment: the github iod reports it itself (heard, next) when the comment replies to a question it is asking, and the person asks for verify for a comment on any issue or pull request the iod watches. verse account -link github asks for verify until the comment is read, so the person need not find a question to reply to.
  • A nonce heard. The same words, said where a trusted iod hears them, bind the account that said them. The iod itself reports it (heard). The Slack iod hears /verse link <nonce> in a message from a member, and names slack:<their id>. The github iod reads it in a comment, and names github:<the author's number>. The iod is verified by its own token and trusted for the provider, as for the relay. The binding's claimant is whoever claimed, as they were authenticated then, and its proof is {method: heard, iod, account, at}.
  • A GitHub claim names a login. The nonce said by any other login binds nothing, and the claim stands.
  • A Slack claim names no account. The nonce binds whoever says it, because a person says it from their own account, as they would use a credential.
  • A nonce nobody claimed binds nobody.
  • Trusted iods. Verse reads a comment only under the root of an iod the deployment trusts, and only an iod the deployment trusts relays a person. The deployment names a provider's iods in its config, auth.principal.providers.<provider>.iods. At every start verse writes each list to verse.identity.<provider>.trust, where anyone can read it. Nothing else writes it. Where no iod is trusted for github, a claim and a verify are refused with 409.
  • One account is one principal. Proving an account that is bound to somebody else is refused with 409, until they revoke it. Proving it again for the same principal answers 200. One principal may hold many accounts.
  • Who reads what. A claim is read by its principal. A binding is read by its principal and by agent:*, so an iod can look an author up. Anyone else asking who gets 404.
  • Where they stand. Claims are at verse.identity.<provider>.claim.<nonce>, and bindings are at verse.identity.<provider>.account.<sub>. A sub is one segment of the address, whatever characters its issuer put in it. A binding's proof is one of:

The root, and the config#

The root's record stands at verse.perms.root. Its child-grants are the grants a node created with no record above it starts with: {read: ["*"]} unless the config says otherwise.

verse has the system's record, with no grants. That record keeps the root's grants out of verse's own half.

Who owns the root, how the config sets it, and the rows verse writes at start (the seed) are in the root and the config, in the repository.

verse perms#

verse perms                      the records of every node you may read
verse perms --root               the root's record
verse perms <path>               that node's record, to anyone who may read the node
verse perms <path> -grant '{read: [ana@example.com]}' [-below]    add a line
verse perms <path> -revoke '{read: [ana@example.com]}'            take one back
verse perms <path> -grants '[…]'                                  replace the node's grants
verse perms <path> -child-grants '{…}'                            the grants what is created beneath starts with

Setting a record on a node that has none takes the place first (above). A node created without a record of its own keeps no copy of the child-grants it was created under: it is its owner's through the place. So closing the place closes what is already in it.

refused refusals, by level

unparsed grantee names on a record that the grammar does not read. Each admits nobody, and versed logs each once per record. On a verse whose records were all written under the grammar, unparsed is zero.

Suspending#

verse flow suspend -charter … -subject … is a control write (control). Verse checks who may make one twice.

When it is written, the writer must hold control over the charter it names, or at the subject it names. Either one is enough. The charter is a package's address, or a rule's own record. For a selector with * in it, verse checks the part before the first *. Verse refuses a suspension that names neither.

When it applies: every time work would go on, verse checks the scope's records at that moment. A suspension covers the work while its holder holds control over the work's charter or at its subject. So a wildcard does not reach further as new owners appear beneath it. A check that cannot be answered covers the work.

A rule the system installed is covered only by a system holder. When a holder loses that authority, the suspension stops covering new work at once. What it already held stays held until somebody resumes it.

Scopes#

Baseline's grants apply in a scope. Verse reads a scope's records through the scope's own view of the store, which lies over baseline. So every grant in baseline stands in the scope, and a record that changes in baseline is seen in every open scope at once.

A grant made in a scope is copy-on-write, like any write to the world. verse perms -scope me, and the record every creation in a scope leaves, land in the scope's layer. They apply to the scope's writes on top of baseline's, and baseline never sees them. Two scopes may hold different owners for one address. Dropping a scope drops its grants.

A task or a gate cannot be acted on from a scope that did not create it (what a scope owns). Grants layer and tasks do not because a grant has no live part. A task is a handle and a gate is an awaiter, and each runs in one engine. A grant is a record that a cache reads, and a cache layers as records do.

Schedules#

This page describes schedules: the records that make time a cause that a rule can fire on. It says what a schedule writes, how a rule reads it, and how a charter declares its own. The charter reference is the model of a rule.

Schedules: time as a cause#

A rule fires on a commit. So for time to be a cause, somebody must write it down.

A schedule is a record in verse's own half, verse.schedule.<name>. It holds its period, its phase and next. next is the instant that the daemon is armed against.

When the timer fires, the daemon writes one commit, and it writes as the schedule. The commit does two things:

  • next advances to the next point on the grid.
  • The tick that is due replaces the standing tick of the schedule.

The address of a tick is the schedule and the instant: verse.schedule.hourly.tick.2026-09-18T03:00:00Z. Nothing else ever writes there.

A rule takes the tick as its subject. Here a team that keeps its code in acme/app has an agent post, each hour, what landed on main in the hour before:

- name: hourly-summary
  condition:
    subject: {path: "verse.schedule.hourly.tick.*"}
  action:
    spawn:
      agent: {name: verse}
      prompt: "post to #deploys a summary of what landed on main in the hour before $[subject.name]"
      environment:
        systems:
          network: {mode: open}
          git: {repo: acme/app, fetch: {}}
          slack: {post: {channel: deploys}}
      autonomy:
      - then: allow

subject.name is the tick's last segment, the instant it is due.

A rule about a tick needs no latch, and may not write one. Each tick has an address of its own, and only the next tick replaces it. So the rule fires once for each tick, and so does every rule that reads the schedule.

Part of the rule How it behaves about a tick
a spawn It is once-ever, and not only while live. The address of a tick never recurs. So a task record that stands for the id means that the spawn happened, and a restart that fires the standing tick again starts nothing.
a gate that was denied It stands on one tick until the next tick replaces that one. Then verse withdraws it as moot.
a completion hook It records its outcome by derive into the charter's own place. It cannot patch the tick, because the tick belongs to the schedule.

The tick carries these fields.

Field What it holds
due the instant at which the tick is due
missed how many instants before it went unticked. A verse that was down ticks once when it comes up, and missed says how late.
date, time, year, month, day, weekday, timezone the calendar of the due instant, in the daemon's zone (TZ)

The calendar takes the place of a cron line. Every verse installs a charter of its own when it starts (internal/versed/system.tony). It has four schedules and two rules.

The four schedules are minutely, hourly, daily and weekly. Their phase is a Monday at midnight in the zone.

The two rules derive one record for each calendar fact.

The rule reads The rule derives
the minutely tick verse.schedule.time, to the minute
the hourly tick .date, .weekday and the rest

A derive of the same date writes the same bytes, and it is a no-op. So date commits once a day, and a rule that reads it wakes once a day. time costs two commits a minute: the tick and the derive. Those commits wake nothing unless a charter reads there.

A rule whose on names a calendar fact rises when the fact becomes true:

    on:                        # daily at five past nine
      path: "verse.schedule.time"
      value: {time: "09:05"}

Downtime has two readings. A rule says which reading it means by what it reads.

Take a verse that is down from 08:30 to 10:30. It writes the 10:00 hourly tick with missed: 1, and the 10:30 minutely tick with missed: 119. time goes from 08:30 to 10:30, and 09:05 never rises.

A rule on Behaves as What it does about a missed instant
the calendar strict cron It skips the instant.
the tick anacron It fires late, once, and missed says by how much.

A charter declares a schedule of its own in its schedules:, beside its strata:. The schedule belongs to the installer. Verse writes its ticks with the grant of the installer, and removes them with the charter. This is the start of the charter that proposes a next step for each issue of the verse repository (issue-next.tony):

at: repos.verse.git-issue
reads: [git-issue, verse.schedule]
schedules:
- name: sweep
  every: 3h
  # from: 2026-01-01T02:00:00Z   the phase: ticks stand at from + n*every; default is Monday 00:00

A package's schedule carries its address, as a package's rule does: verse.schedule."repos.verse.git-issue:sweep".

A period of whole days steps by calendar days. So a nightly schedule keeps its wall-clock time across a DST change.

Command What it does
verse schedule lists every schedule, with its next and its standing tick
verse schedule tick <name> --due <instant> [--at <address>] writes one tick for a past instant. It writes the same data that the schedule writes, so a rule about the tick fires in the same way. --at names the package of a schedule a charter declared.

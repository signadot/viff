Permissions#

This page is the model of permissions. It defines the words and states the rules. Two other pages go with it:

  • Permissions, walked shows the model by example, from data of your own to a gate that a resolver answers.
  • The permissions reference has every field, rule and table.

The model has three rules:

  • Every node in the store has one owner.
  • The owner, and the owners of the nodes above it, may do anything to the node.
  • Everybody else may do what a grant lets them do.

The rest of this page says what those words mean, in the order you need them.

The words#

Word Meaning
node anything with an address: demo.pr.1, a field of it (demo.pr.1.stage), or a rule (verse.trigger.rule.review). An address has steps, so every node has nodes above it, up to the root.
principal who is acting: a person, an agent, a runner, or verse itself
record what verse keeps about a node: its owner, the grants on it, and the grants that a node created beneath it starts with. Only verse writes records. You read and set your own records with verse perms.
permission level what somebody may do at a node: read, read-write or owner. Each of the three includes the one before it. control is a fourth level beside them. It lets somebody steer work: run, cancel, pause, resume, say, change an autonomy, and suspend.
grant a line on a node's record. It gives a permission level to somebody who is not the node's owner.

On this page, "level" means a permission level. It does not mean the level of a stratum.

A refusal may say that somebody holds read at a node. This means that read is the most the records give them at that node, as owner or by grant. The word holds means nothing more.

Principals and grantees#

A principal is a name with its kind in front, such as user:ana@example.com. A person is user:, an agent is agent:, and a runner is runner:. Verse itself is system:. Something installed that acts, such as a rule, is via:<address> (what acts on its own).

A grantee is who a grant is to. A grantee is a principal, or a set of principals: * for everyone, <kind>:* for every principal of a kind, group:<name> for a group's members, audience:<address> for whoever holds the same level at that address, or answering:<address> for the resolvers answering for it. The grammar is in the reference.

Every request names who makes it. The CLI sends the user: or agent: in its config. On the commands that take it, the flag -as names somebody else. Verse refuses a request that writes and names nobody.

A principal is what the request says, unless the verse verifies it. By default, verse trusts a name as much as it trusts -as, and no more. A deployment can name the identity providers its people sign in with. A request that carries a token from one of them is then made as the person the token proves. Verse refuses a token that proves nobody. The rest of the model is the same either way: every act names a party, and verse checks the act against what that party may do. Who a request is from is in the reference.

Ownership#

The owner of a node is the nearest record at or above it that names one. Most nodes have no record of their own. For example, demo.pr.1.stage is owned by whoever owns demo.pr.1, or demo.pr, or demo.

You own what you create. A write that brings nodes into being leaves one record. The record names the writer, and it stands at the topmost node that the write made. Write demo.pr.1 into an empty store, and demo is yours, with everything beneath it. You may create where you own the node above. You may also create where a grant says you may (child-owner, below).

A node's first grants are child-grants, or its creator's. A node you create starts with the grants that the nearest child-grants above it names. You can also give it grants of your own as you create it. verse data notes ana todo -put '{…}' -grants '[]' makes a note that nobody else reads, from its first commit. The node and its record land together, so the node is never readable more widely than you said (creating with grants).

Owners above co-own. Whoever owns demo may do anything to demo.pr.1, whoever made it.

Ownership moves only by creating and deleting. Verse has no transfer. To give something to somebody, they make it. Deleting a node forgets its record and every record beneath it. Whoever creates there next starts with no record.

A place can be taken before anything goes in it. Run verse perms spaces you -grants '[]' where nothing stands yet, and the place is yours. Without -grants, the command only reads the record. A node that already stands belongs to its owner, and the command does not take it from them. A place goes to whoever takes it first (creating and taking a place).

Grants#

# the record of spaces.you, as `verse perms spaces you` shows it
owner: user:you@example.com
grants:
- read: [user:ana@example.com]        # this node and everything beneath it
- below: true                         # beneath it only, not the node itself
  read-write: [group:payments]
- child-owner: [user:*]               # may create beneath it, and own what they create
child-grants: {read: ["*"]}           # the grants a node created beneath starts with

Know three things about grants before the rest:

  • A grant stops at the next owner's record. A grant on spaces reaches everything beneath spaces that the owner of spaces still owns. It does not reach into spaces.you once you own it. You decide what is granted there.
  • child-owner is the right to create, and nothing else. It lets somebody make new nodes beneath a node and own them. It gives them nothing over what already stands there. It never lets anybody take the node above a place that somebody else has made.
  • child-grants is copied when a node is created. The new node's record starts with it, unless the creator gives grants of their own. A later change to child-grants changes what is created later. It changes nothing that exists.

A group is a named set of grantees with an owner (verse group add payments ana@…). Verse reads the members of a group when it checks a grant. So membership changes without a change to the grants that name the group.

What acts on its own#

Four things act when nobody typed anything:

  • a rule fires;
  • an iod writes what a system holds, or carries a rule's write back out to it;
  • a resolver answers a gate;
  • a schedule ticks.

One rule covers all four: an installed thing acts as whoever installed it. Its writes appear as via:<its address>. The check treats that principal as the owner of the installed thing's record.

A rule can do exactly what its installer can do, and it sees exactly what its installer can see. An iod writes into the store as its installer, into places its installer owns.

Verse refuses an install over somebody else's place. Verse also refuses an install that would leave no owner on record. A thing with no owner acts as nobody, so it would stand there and do nothing.

The charter a deployment ships is installed as the system, and acts as the system (installed things and their records, in the repository).

A spawn with as: subject acts for the owner of the record that fired it. Its task acts as pair:<owner> via:<the rule's address>, and may do what the owner may. The pair names the rule so that the record says what the task acted through. It adds no authority and takes none away. A rule may say as: subject only over a place its installer owns. So a rule cannot act as somebody whose records it only selects (as: subject). A pair is a principal and never a grantee: nothing is granted to one.

The top of the tree#

The root is a node with a record, like any other node. verse perms --root shows it. In an open verse, anybody may make a top-level place, and owns what they make. Everyone may read it. A deployment may allow only admins to make a top-level place. Then everybody else works in places that an admin opens to them (the root and the config, in the repository).

Verse's own half#

Everything under verse belongs to the system: rules, tasks, runners, iods, resolvers, schedules, open questions, and the records themselves. You do not write there directly. You make things with the verbs that own them (charter -a, task run, iod add, question approve). What a verb makes for you is yours.

In an open verse, anybody may use those verbs. Anybody may read what stands under verse, except the records, which you read with verse perms. A rule may watch verse's own half and react to it. A rule may not write into it.

Tasks and gates#

A task belongs to its spawner. The runner that works the task is granted read-write and control on that task and nothing else.

A task may act on verse itself, as its owner would, through the verse system. The runner carries each such request with its own token, and names the task. Verse admits it as the task's owner, and only while the task runs (a task carried by its runner). The runner gains nothing by it: the request may do what the owner may, and no more.

These principals may answer a gate's question:

  • whoever owns what asked it: the task's owner, or the charter's owner;
  • whoever the gate names as answerers, each of whom must be able to read what the question is about;
  • where the gate names nobody, whoever may write what the question is about, or any person or agent where it is about nothing.

Nobody answers a question about something they cannot read. A question about something is read by whoever may read that thing.

A resolver answers a question only when the question is held out to that resolver. The runner that works a task cannot answer that task's questions. The runner's grant is on the task, and a question is not part of the task.

Verse records an answer, a say, a cancel or a change of autonomy as made by whoever made the request. A remote worker reports on its own task: its status, its output, the decisions it saw. That report never decides who answered a question or what they chose.

What you see#

A refused write or read is a 403. The refusal says three things:

  • what the requester holds;
  • what the act needed;
  • which record decided.
refused: user:bob@example.com holds read at demo.pr.1 and writing it needs read-write;
the nearest owner on record is user:alice@example.com at demo

Verse refuses a read of one thing you may not read. A listing, a watch or a replay leaves it out. So you see the same set whichever way you ask.

You read and set records with verse perms.

Suspending#

Suspending work (verse flow suspend) needs control over the charter it names, or at the subject it names. A suspension covers only the work that its holder has control over when the work would run. So a wide selector does not reach into places that somebody else comes to own. Control has the commands, and the reference has the rule in full.

Scopes#

Baseline's records also apply in a scope. Opening a scope is not a way around a grant. A grant made in a scope is copy-on-write, like anything else written there. It applies in that scope, baseline never sees it, and it goes when the scope goes. The reference says why grants layer this way and tasks do not.

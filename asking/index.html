Asking#

verse ask asks somebody something. The ask goes to the people you name, with a date if you give one. Their answer is written back on your ask, and what you said should follow that answer runs for you. Every verse can ask: nothing has to be installed for it.

An ask is how anything in verse waits for a person. You raise one with verse ask. A rule's gate raises one when it stops for a person. A task raises one with its question-ask tool and waits on it, or with verse-ask-create as the person it runs for. All of them are the same record, on one docket.

The words#
ask a record at verse.ask.<id>: what is asked, of whom, by when, and what follows. verse ask writes yours, and its id comes from the name you give it
asker whoever raised the ask: its record's from, {principal: <you>}. Your ask is your record
to whom the ask is put to: a name or a list, in the grammar a grant uses. Absent, anyone may answer. The record keeps it as to: {grantees: […]}
due the date the ask settles at if nobody answers. Absent, it has no date and waits for an answer
on silence the option the ask settles on at its date if nobody answers: deny, unless you say --on-silence proceed
question an ask as the person asked sees it: one that waits on you. verse question lists them
docket every pending ask you can read, whoever raised it and whoever it waits on. verse question --all lists it
on what follows the answer: a list of rows, read in order. Each row is a match on the answer and a then. The first row whose match the answer meets runs its then (what follows). Absent, or met by no row, the ask is a vote
then what a row runs, as one key: {spawn: {…}} is a task, written as a charter's spawn is (prompt or script, agent, environment, autonomy, …) and meaning only what it says; {answer: {ask: <id>, choice: <option>}} answers another ask as you; {ask: {…}} raises another ask. null or {} runs nothing
answer what is written back: yes or no
ran what the row that met the answer ran: {row, at} and the task it started, the answer or ask it made, or why it failed
edit which version of your ask an answer agreed to: 1 as first asked, and one more each time you change what it asks
labels what the ask says about itself, for whoever watches asks: --label team=payments,kind=deploy. Verse routes nothing by them

Every question on the docket, yours or not, says its parties the same way. from is who raised it, exactly one of {rule, at} (a rule's gate, and the charter it is installed at), {task} (a task's question), {principal} (an ask, or words said to a task: a person, an agent, or a resolver acting as via:…) or {principal, task} (an ask a task raised as the person it runs for). to is whom it is put to, {grantees: […]} or, for words said to a task, {task}, and is absent where it names nobody. by is who settled it.

Asking#

cat > on.tony <<'EOF'
- match: {verdict: proceed}
  then:
    spawn:
      prompt: fast-forward main to ask-ui, run the tests, and push main
      environment:
        systems:
          network: {mode: open}
          commands: {}
          git:
          - repo: acme/app
            from: main
            fetch: {}
            push: {ref: refs/heads/main, force: false, delete: false}
      autonomy:
      - {match: {system: commands}, then: allow}
      - {match: {system: git, verb: fetch}, then: allow}
      - {then: ask}
EOF
verse ask land -q "Merge ask-ui into main of acme/app? No answer in 48h is a yes." \
  --to dana@example.com --due 48h --on-silence proceed --on @on.tony

on.tony has one row. Its match, {verdict: proceed}, meets a yes, and its then is what follows: an agent given the prompt, in an environment that gives it the network, the shell, and a checkout of acme/app that it may fetch and push to main without a rewrite. The autonomy runs the shell and the fetch unasked, and asks you before the push. A spawn means only what it says: one that names no network has none, and verse checks it against the runner it would run on when you ask, not after the yes.

verse ask answers with the ask's id: land- and a suffix. Commands take the name or the id. If you have used one name for two asks, verse refuses the name and prints both ids, so you can pick one.

The ask goes to Dana. It is on the docket, and in Slack where the deployment runs the Slack iod. Dana answers it like any ask: verse question <id> --answer proceed for yes, or deny for no. You do not answer your own ask, unless you named yourself in --to.

--due takes a date (2026-10-02T17:00:00Z) or a duration from now. If Dana answers yes, the ask gets answer: yes, and the row's then runs as a task for you. A no gets answer: no, meets no row, and runs nothing. No answer by the date settles on deny, unless you said otherwise. Here silence means nobody objects, so --on-silence proceed makes silence a yes. You choose it when you ask, and it stays when the date moves.

Leave out --to, and anyone may answer. Leave out --on, and the ask is a vote: the answer is all it leaves.

A then that is a script runs exactly what it says, as verse task run runs one:

cat > release.tony <<'EOF'
- match: {verdict: proceed}
  then:
    spawn:
      script: |
        git -C app merge --ff-only mirror/main
        verse-call-tool git-push -repo app -branch release
      environment:
        systems:
          network: {mode: open}
          git:
          - repo: acme/app
            from: release
            fetch: {}
            push: {ref: refs/heads/release, force: false, delete: false}
      autonomy:
      - {then: allow}
EOF
verse ask release -q "Fast-forward release to main for v1.2?" --to dana@example.com --on @release.tony

The script fast-forwards the checkout's release to main, and the runner pushes it. A push is the runner's act, with the runner's credential, so the script asks for it with verse-call-tool git-push, and the git block gives that one push.

A prompt lets an agent choose its own actions, so that task's own rows ask you. A script is what you wrote, so its autonomy lets it run as you wrote it.

A then may also answer another ask, or raise one, as you (composing asks).

Verse checks what you give before it writes anything: the names, the date, that each match is a pattern it can apply, and that each then is one key an ask can run. If it refuses, nothing is written.

What follows the answer#

on is a list of rows. When the ask settles, verse reads the rows in order, and the first row whose match meets the answer runs its then. No later row runs. A row with no match meets every answer, so it belongs last. A row whose then is null or {} runs nothing, and the rows after it do not run either.

The match is a tony match pattern, the one verse data --if takes. It is matched against the answer as the ask's record holds it under followed:

field what it is
verdict the option the ask settled on: proceed or deny
by who settled it, or deadline where silence did at the date
via the resolver or io the answer came through, where one did
why, say the reason given and the words said with the answer, where there were any

So {verdict: proceed} meets a yes, and {verdict: deny} meets a no, answered or by silence. {by: deadline} meets silence alone, and {verdict: !not proceed} meets anything but a yes. Rows can tell an answered no from silence:

- match: {verdict: proceed}
  then: {spawn: {…}}
- match: {by: deadline}
  then: {ask: {name: ship-lead, question: "Nobody answered: ship the hotfix?", to: [lead@example.com]}}

A yes runs the first row. Silence at the date runs the second. An answered no meets neither, so it runs nothing.

Composing asks#

A then may be one more act of yours: an answer to another ask, or another ask. Verse makes it as you when the answer meets its row. It gives you no right you do not already have. The answer is only the condition.

Your answer waits on somebody else's. Alice asks you to ship the hotfix. You will say yes, but only once Dana has looked at the UI:

# alice
verse ask ship -q "Ship the hotfix?" --to you@example.com
#   raised question ship-3f2a…: Ship the hotfix?

# you
verse ask ui-ok -q "Is the UI ok to ship?" --to dana@example.com --due 24h \
  --on '[{match: {verdict: proceed}, then: {answer: {ask: ship-3f2a…, choice: proceed, say: "dana checked the UI"}}}]'

When Dana answers proceed, verse answers ship with proceed as you, with your words, as if you had answered it yourself. verse ask ui-ok says what happened:

answer: yes
…
ran:
  row: 0
  at: "2026-09-30T10:12:00Z"
  answered:
    ask: ship-3f2a…
    at: "2026-09-30T10:12:00Z"
    status: 204

Alice's verse ask ship reads answer: yes, and its record says it was answered by: user:you@example.com. If Dana says no, or nobody answers by the date, nothing is answered, and ship still waits on you. If ship needs two proceeds, yours is one ballot, and it waits for another.

Asks chain. {ask: {…}} takes what verse ask takes (name, question, to, due, onSilence, on, about, labels, keep), and raises that ask as you when its row is met. A due that is a duration counts from the answer. Its own rows may answer or ask in turn. Here Dana's yes asks Ann to deploy, and Ann's yes answers ship:

verse ask ui-ok -q "Is the UI ok to ship?" --to dana@example.com --due 24h \
  --on '[{match: {verdict: proceed}, then: {ask: {name: deploy, question: "Deploy the hotfix to staging?",
          to: [ann@example.com], due: 4h,
          on: [{match: {verdict: proceed}, then: {answer: {ask: ship-3f2a…, choice: proceed}}}]}}}]'

verse ask ui-ok then reads ran: {row: 0, asked: {id: deploy-…}}. deploy is your ask, to Ann, due four hours after Dana's yes.

Verse checks when you ask, and again when it acts. When you ask ui-ok, verse checks that ship exists, waits on you, and has a proceed option, and that Ann can read what deploy is about. If any check fails, verse refuses the ask and writes nothing. When the yes comes, verse makes the answer or the ask as you would make it at that moment:

  • The answer is to ship as you saw it. If Alice changes ship before Dana answers, ship is asked again, and your answer to the old words is refused.
  • If ship has settled in the meantime, there is nothing to answer.

What was refused, and why, is on your ask, under ran, as answered or asked with refused.

A no can run something too. This is how you escalate. Ask Ann, and if she says no or nobody answers in four hours, ask the lead:

verse ask ship -q "Ship the hotfix?" --to ann@example.com --due 4h \
  --on '[{match: {verdict: deny}, then: {ask: {name: ship-lead, question: "Ann said no, or did not answer: ship the hotfix?",
          to: [lead@example.com], due: 4h}}}]'

verse ask ship then reads ran: {row: 0, asked: {id: ship-lead-…}}. An ask withdrawn before anybody answered runs nothing. The message in Slack shows each row: what it meets, and what it runs.

How long it stands#

An ask that has settled, answered or not, stays for a week and is then removed. That holds for every ask on the docket, whoever raised it. For your own ask, --keep 72h sets another time. verse ask shows when it goes, as expires. A task its row started stays as tasks do.

An ask settles once, on the first answer that settles it. Verse writes that answer on the ask as followed before the row it meets runs, and before whoever asked hears it. So what runs, what the asker hears and what the ask says are that one answer's. An answer that lands after it is refused, and the refusal names whose answer stands. If the answer cannot reach whoever waits on the ask, followed says why, as undelivered. The answer still stands: it reaches them when the ask is asked again, and answering it again meanwhile says the answer stands.

If a rule's gate fires again after its ask settled, it raises a new ask with its own id. The settled ask keeps its answer until it goes. An ask that is asked again while it still waits, because a restart or a lifted suspension brings its gate back, is not removed: it stays while it waits.

Who reads it#

You read your ask, and so do the people it asks. Nobody else does. An ask that names nobody, which anyone may answer, is read by everyone.

An ask --about something is read by whoever may read that thing instead. If it also names nobody, whoever may write that thing may answer it. You, and everybody you ask, must be able to read what the ask is about, or verse refuses it.

Following your asks#

verse ask                # your asks: the question, who is asked, the date, the answer or pending
verse ask land           # one of them, and whom it waits on
verse ask --watch        # a line for each event, until stopped

An ask that settles with nobody to answer it, because verse could not put it to whom it names, has no answer: it shows its status, orphaned, and why.

--watch prints a line when an ask of yours is asked, asked again, answered or withdrawn. Each line starts with its revision. Pass the last one you saw as --since on the next run, and verse replays what happened in between. The store can replay for as long as it keeps its full log: an hour by default (verse up --logd-config changes it).

What others ask of you is the other side, verse question:

verse question            # the asks waiting on you, that you did not raise
verse question <id>       # one of them, with its options and ballots
verse question --watch    # a line when one starts waiting on you, and when it settles

verse question leaves out an ask that a resolver answers for you; --served adds it (resolvers). --all lists the whole docket. verse question --watch takes --since as verse ask --watch does.

Either watch takes --notify to say what needs you on the desktop too: a question that starts waiting on you (urgent, so it stays until you dismiss it where the desktop keeps such ones), and an ask of yours answered. A question that settles closes its notification rather than adding one; the watch's line says how it settled. What stands when the watch opens, or what --since and a reconnect catch up on, is one quiet summary ("2 questions wait on you"), not a notification each; the summary is closed once every question it counted has settled.

On Linux and the BSDs it goes to the notification service on the session bus (org.freedesktop.Notifications, which GNOME, KDE, dunst, mako and swaync serve), with a question's text escaped where the service reads markup. On macOS it goes to Notification Center through osascript, which attributes it to Script Editor: allow Script Editor under System Settings › Notifications, or nothing appears. With no such service the watch refuses to start. A desktop that refuses or falls behind is said once, on a line; the lines are the same either way.

verse question --watch --notify

Changing your mind#

While an ask waits, you can change it, or take it back:

verse ask land --due 72h                               # move its date: nothing is asked again
verse ask land --on @on.tony                           # change what follows: it is asked again
verse ask land --to peggy@example.com                    # ask somebody else: asked again, of them
verse ask land --withdraw                              # take it back

An answer agrees to what was asked, not to when. So a new date moves the clock and keeps any answer already given. A new --on or a new --to changes what is asked, so the ask is asked again as its next edit. Answers to the earlier edit do not count, and an answer that names that edit is refused. So nobody answers words they were not asked.

Withdrawing an ask takes it off the docket and removes it. Nothing is written on anybody's behalf.

Once an ask has an answer, it does not change, even before the answer reaches you. Anything more is a new ask.

In Slack, the message says who asked and what each row runs, and a reply answers that edit. The message is drawn again when the ask changes. A reply to the old words is refused. A reply to the new message answers what it now says.

Whose authority what follows runs with#

Yours alone. You raised the ask, so what follows it is yours to answer for. Ask Dana "may I merge this?", and her yes lets you merge what you may merge. Dana does not need that right. The task is your record, so its own gates ask you.

Nobody but you writes your ask. It is yours, and nobody owns a place above it.

What follows runs before your wait is released, and a spawn has run once it is launched.

A task's verse-ask-create is your ask too, when the task runs for you. Its rows run with your authority, so a spawn in any of them is held within the task: its environment no wider than the task's, less installing charters (verse.charter-install), its autonomy no looser, on the task's runner, spending the task's credential where it spends one (a script spends none). The same holds for an ask a row raises, and for its rows. An answer in any of them is refused: it would be yours, on any ask that waits on you, another task's gate among them. A task cannot raise past what you let it do. Once raised, the ask is yours to change as any of yours.

Only an ask has an on. You need not be there when the answer comes, so the ask carries what follows it. A rule's gate has none: what a rule does about a yes is its action (gates). An autonomy row's ask has none either: the task waits on it, and what the task does about a yes is the step it asked about (tasks).

Saying something to a running task#

Words you say to a running task are an ask to the task:

verse task say run-7 "use the staging bucket"     # said to run-7: say-3f2a…
verse ask say-3f2a…                               # answer: heard, once the task has taken them

The ask is yours, about the task, and whoever may read the task may read it. It has no date: a turn in a conversation has none. If the task is waiting for your turn, the words are your reply, and the turn is answered. Otherwise the task takes them at its next boundary, and the ask settles as heard. If the task ends before it takes them, the ask is withdrawn, and says so. Words to a task on a remote runner are heard when verse passes them to the runner. verse flow say says the words to each task the flow reaches, as one ask to each.

Nobody else answers these asks, so they are not on the docket.
